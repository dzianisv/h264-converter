How to convert videos offline on Android without ads

Most "free" video converters either upload your video to a server or make you watch an ad to convert a file. Neither is necessary: conversion is just repackaging or re-encoding streams, and a phone can do it on its own. Here's how to convert offline, with no ads and no account.

November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

First: how to tell if a converter can upload your video

An app can only upload your video if it has network access. On Android, check the app's permissions before you trust it: Settings → Apps → the app → Permissions. If it asks for network access, it can send your files somewhere — whether it does is a promise, not a guarantee. An app with no network permission physically cannot upload anything, which is the only check that doesn't rely on trust.

On a computer the same test is simpler: FFmpeg works with no network at all. Check what's inside your file first — it takes one command, and it tells you what the conversion needs to do:

ffprobe -v error -show_entries stream=codec_name,codec_type -of default=noprint_wrappers=1 input.mp4

If the codec is already the one you want, the conversion is a copy, not a re-encode — seconds, no quality loss. Only re-encode when the codec itself has to change.

Method 1: FFmpeg on your computer (fully offline)

FFmpeg is the free, open-source tool that does the actual converting inside most converter apps. Install it once from ffmpeg.org (or brew install ffmpeg on a Mac, sudo apt install ffmpeg on Ubuntu/Debian), then run it with no network needed — you can even disconnect and it still works:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c copy output.mp4

-c copy means "copy every stream, don't re-encode" — same video, same audio, new file in seconds. If the codec has to change, re-encode to the most compatible pair, H.264 video with AAC audio:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c:v libx264 -crf 20 -c:a aac output.mp4

-crf 20 is a good quality setting for H.264 — lower is better quality and a bigger file; 18–23 is the sensible range for most videos. Nothing here touches the internet: the file goes in, the file comes out, and no server ever sees it.

Method 2: on your phone, without ads

The same job runs on Android, on-device, if the app is built that way. Convert264 converts video on the phone itself — free, no ads, no account — and it has no network permission, so your video physically cannot leave the device. Pick a video, choose the format, and the conversion happens locally, even in airplane mode.

What to avoid

  • Online converters. They upload your video to someone else's server, convert it there, and send it back. That's slow for big files, and your video — possibly a private one — sits on a server you don't control. For anything you wouldn't post publicly, convert locally.
  • Free converter apps full of ads. When we sampled the newest 1-star reviews of the top video converters on Google Play, the same complaints kept coming up: ads interrupting the conversion, formats locked behind a paywall, and outputs that come out corrupt or unplayable. If an app makes you watch an ad to convert a file, it's selling your attention, not converting your video.

Rule of thumb: if the converter needs the internet, ask what the internet is for. Conversion doesn't. An app that converts offline and shows no ads has nothing to sell you — and nothing of yours to sell either.
