How to convert video to H.264 without uploading it

H.264 is the codec almost every phone, player, editor, and TV understands — it's why a video that plays everywhere is almost always H.264 inside an MP4. If your file is VP9, HEVC, or something older, converting it to H.264 takes one command, and your video never has to leave your computer.

November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

First: check whether your video is already H.264

H.264 is a codec — the way the picture itself is compressed — not a container like MP4 or WebM. A file can already be H.264 and still refuse to play somewhere, because it's in the wrong container; or it can be in an MP4 and still be VP9 inside. Before you re-encode anything, check what you actually have:

ffprobe -v error -select_streams v:0 -show_entries stream=codec_name -of csv=p=0 input.mp4

If it prints h264, your video is already H.264 — don't re-encode it, just copy the streams into an MP4 container (ffmpeg -i input.mkv -c copy output.mp4), which takes seconds and loses no quality. Re-encoding is only for when the codec itself has to change, which is what the rest of this page is about.

Method 1: FFmpeg (fastest, one command)

FFmpeg is the free, open-source tool that does the actual converting inside most converter apps. Install it once from ffmpeg.org (or brew install ffmpeg on a Mac, sudo apt install ffmpeg on Ubuntu/Debian), then run:

ffmpeg -i input.webm -c:v libx264 -crf 20 -c:a aac output.mp4

That works for any input — WebM, MOV, MKV, AVI, whatever you have. -c:v libx264 encodes the video to H.264, -crf 20 sets the quality (lower is better quality and a bigger file; 18–23 is the sensible range for most videos), and -c:a aac converts the audio to AAC, the format that pairs with H.264 in an MP4. If your audio is already AAC, you can copy it instead with -c:a copy and skip re-encoding the sound entirely.

One note: re-encoding always loses a little quality — you're compressing an already-compressed picture a second time. At CRF 20 the loss is hard to see, but don't re-encode the same file twice if you can avoid it. Keep the original, and convert from that original every time you need a new copy.

Method 2: VLC (no command line)

If you'd rather click than type, VLC can do the same job:

  1. Open VLC, go to Media → Convert / Save.
  2. Click Add, pick your video file, then Convert / Save.
  3. Under Profile, choose Video – H.264 + MP3 (MP4).
  4. Click Browse, choose where to save the .mp4, and press Start.

VLC re-encodes by default, so it's slower than the FFmpeg copy — but it works on any file, and there's nothing to install beyond VLC itself.

What to avoid

  • Online converters. They upload your video to someone else's server, convert it there, and send it back. That's slow for big files, and your video — possibly a private one — sits on a server you don't control. For anything you wouldn't post publicly, convert locally.
  • Free converter apps full of ads. When we sampled the newest 1-star reviews of the top video converters on Google Play, the same complaints kept coming up: ads interrupting the conversion, formats locked behind a paywall, and outputs that come out corrupt or unplayable. If an app makes you watch an ad to convert a file, it's selling your attention, not converting your video.

Rule of thumb: if the file is already H.264, copy the streams — never re-encode. Re-encoding is for when the codec itself has to change, and H.264 is the codec to change to when you want the file to play everywhere.
