How to trim a video on Android without uploading it

Every video has dead air at the start or the end — the seconds before you pressed record properly, the fumble after the moment passed. Trimming cuts those off, and you can do it without uploading your video anywhere.

November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

First: find your cut points

Play the video and note the two timestamps you want to keep: where the good part starts and where it ends. Write them down as hours:minutes:seconds — for example, keep from 00:00:02 to 00:00:05. Check the total length first, so you know the numbers are inside the video:

ffprobe -v error -show_entries format=duration -of csv=p=0 input.mp4

It prints the duration in seconds. Your end point has to be smaller than that number.

Method 1: FFmpeg, exact to the second (re-encode)

FFmpeg is the free, open-source tool that does the actual converting inside most converter apps. Install it once from ffmpeg.org (or brew install ffmpeg on a Mac, sudo apt install ffmpeg on Ubuntu/Debian), then run one command. Put both -ss (start) and -to (end) after the input file, and the cut lands exactly where you asked:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -ss 00:00:02 -to 00:00:05 -c:v libx264 -crf 20 -c:a aac trimmed.mp4

-crf 20 is a good quality setting for H.264 — lower is better quality and a bigger file; 18–23 is the sensible range for most videos. This re-encodes the kept part, so it takes a little while, but the start and end are frame-accurate: a 2-second to 5-second trim comes out at exactly 3.000 seconds.

Method 2: FFmpeg, fast (no re-encoding)

If you don't want to wait for a re-encode, copy the streams instead. Put -ss before the input file and use -t (duration, not end time) after it:

ffmpeg -ss 00:00:02 -i input.mp4 -t 00:00:03 -c copy trimmed.mp4

-c copy means "copy every stream, don't re-encode" — the trim takes seconds even for a large file. Two things to know: the cut lands on the nearest keyframe, so the result can be a fraction of a second longer (our 3-second trim came out at 3.1 seconds), and with -ss before the input, -to counts from your start point, not from the beginning of the file — so use -t for the length you want to keep. If the exact second matters, use Method 1.

Method 3: VLC (no command line)

If you'd rather click than type, VLC can trim by recording the part you want:

  1. Open VLC, go to View → Advanced Controls so the red record button appears.
  2. Play your video and seek to where the good part starts.
  3. Click the red record button, let it play to where the good part ends, then click record again to stop.
  4. VLC saves the recorded part as a new file in your Videos folder (on Windows, C:\Users\YourName\Videos).

This plays in real time — a 3-minute clip takes 3 minutes to trim — and the cut is only as precise as your clicks. But it works on any file, and there's nothing to install beyond VLC itself.

What to avoid

  • Online trimmers. They upload your video to someone else's server, trim it there, and send it back. That's slow for big files, and your video — possibly a private one — sits on a server you don't control. For anything you wouldn't post publicly, trim locally.
  • Free trimmer apps full of ads and watermarks. When we sampled the newest 1-star reviews of the top video converters on Google Play, the same complaints kept coming up: ads interrupting the conversion, formats locked behind a paywall, and outputs that come out corrupt or unplayable. If an app makes you watch an ad — or stamps its logo on your video — to cut a few seconds off, it's selling your attention, not trimming your video.

Rule of thumb: if the exact second matters, re-encode with -ss and -to after the input. If speed matters and a fraction of a second doesn't, copy with -ss before the input and -t for the length.
