How to convert MKV to MP4 without uploading your video

MKV is a great container — but some phones, TVs, and players refuse it. MP4 plays almost everywhere. The fix takes seconds, and your video never has to leave your computer.

November 14, 2026 · 4 min read

First: you probably don't need to re-encode

MKV and MP4 are containers — boxes that hold the same video and audio streams. In most cases the video inside an MKV is already H.264 and the audio is already AAC, which MP4 accepts as-is. So instead of re-encoding (slow, and it loses a little quality every time), you just copy the streams into a new box. That takes seconds, even for a large file, and the picture is bit-for-bit identical.

Only re-encode if the copy fails or the player still complains — that means the streams inside aren't MP4-compatible, and then you have no choice.

Method 1: FFmpeg (fastest, one command)

FFmpeg is the free, open-source tool that does the actual converting inside most converter apps. Install it once from ffmpeg.org (or brew install ffmpeg on a Mac, sudo apt install ffmpeg on Ubuntu/Debian), then run:

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -c copy output.mp4

That's it. -c copy means "copy every stream, don't re-encode". If FFmpeg complains about an incompatible stream, fall back to re-encoding the video and audio:

ffmpeg -i input.mkv -c:v libx264 -crf 20 -c:a aac output.mp4

-crf 20 is a good quality setting for H.264 — lower is better quality and a bigger file; 18–23 is the sensible range for most videos.

Method 2: VLC (no command line)

If you'd rather click than type, VLC can do the same job:

  1. Open VLC, go to Media → Convert / Save.
  2. Click Add, pick your MKV file, then Convert / Save.
  3. Under Profile, choose Video – H.264 + MP3 (MP4).
  4. Click Browse, choose where to save the .mp4, and press Start.

VLC re-encodes by default, so it's slower than the FFmpeg copy — but it works on any file, and there's nothing to install beyond VLC itself.

What to avoid

  • Online converters. They upload your video to someone else's server, convert it there, and send it back. That's slow for big files, and your video — possibly a private one — sits on a server you don't control. For anything you wouldn't post publicly, convert locally.
  • Free converter apps full of ads. When we sampled the newest 1-star reviews of the top video converters on Google Play, the same complaints kept coming up: ads interrupting the conversion, formats locked behind a paywall, and outputs that come out corrupt or unplayable. If an app makes you watch an ad to convert a file, it's selling your attention, not converting your video.

Rule of thumb: if the file plays fine and you only need a different container, copy the streams — never re-encode. Re-encoding is for when the codec itself has to change.
