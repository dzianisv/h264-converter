How to convert video to MKV without uploading it

MKV is the most forgiving container there is — it holds almost any video and audio codec, plus subtitles and chapters. If a video needs to survive in one file with everything intact, MKV is where it goes. The conversion takes seconds, and your video never has to leave your computer.

November 15, 2026 · 4 min read

First: check what's inside, then copy — don't re-encode

MKV and MP4, MOV, WebM are all containers — boxes that hold the same video and audio streams. MKV (Matroska) accepts nearly every codec, so in most cases the streams inside your file are already fine as they are. Instead of re-encoding (slow, and it loses a little quality every time), you just copy the streams into a new box. That takes seconds, even for a large file, and the picture is bit-for-bit identical.

Check what's inside first — it takes one command, and it tells you whether the copy will work:

ffprobe -v error -show_entries stream=codec_name,codec_type -of default=noprint_wrappers=1 input.mp4

Almost any codec the command lists — H.264, H.265, VP9, AAC, MP3, Opus — is valid inside MKV. Only re-encode if the copy fails or the player you need still complains.

Method 1: FFmpeg (fastest, one command)

FFmpeg is the free, open-source tool that does the actual converting inside most converter apps. Install it once from ffmpeg.org (or brew install ffmpeg on a Mac, sudo apt install ffmpeg on Ubuntu/Debian), then run:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c copy output.mkv

That's it. -c copy means "copy every stream, don't re-encode" — video, audio, and subtitles all come along. If FFmpeg complains about an incompatible stream, fall back to re-encoding the video and audio to the most compatible pair:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c:v libx264 -crf 20 -c:a aac output.mkv

-crf 20 is a good quality setting for H.264 — lower is better quality and a bigger file; 18–23 is the sensible range for most videos. If you'd rather have the smallest file than the most compatible one, encode VP9 video with Opus audio instead — both are right at home in MKV:

ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -c:v libvpx-vp9 -crf 30 -b:v 0 -c:a libopus output.mkv

Method 2: VLC (no command line)

If you'd rather click than type, VLC can do the same job:

  1. Open VLC, go to Media → Convert / Save.
  2. Click Add, pick your video file, then Convert / Save.
  3. Under Profile, choose Video – H.264 + MP3 (MKV).
  4. Click Browse, choose where to save the .mkv, and press Start.

VLC re-encodes by default, so it's slower than the FFmpeg copy — but it works on any file, and there's nothing to install beyond VLC itself.

What to avoid

  • Online converters. They upload your video to someone else's server, convert it there, and send it back. That's slow for big files, and your video — possibly a private one — sits on a server you don't control. For anything you wouldn't post publicly, convert locally.
  • Free converter apps full of ads. When we sampled the newest 1-star reviews of the top video converters on Google Play, the same complaints kept coming up: ads interrupting the conversion, formats locked behind a paywall, and outputs that come out corrupt or unplayable. If an app makes you watch an ad to convert a file, it's selling your attention, not converting your video.

Rule of thumb: MKV is the flexible box — if a codec exists, MKV probably holds it. Copy the streams first and re-encode only when a specific player demands a specific codec. Re-encoding is for when the codec itself has to change, not the container.
